Instrumentl is expensive relative to the category, and whether that's a problem depends entirely on how many applications you have in flight. The tool is a pipeline manager with a matching engine attached. If you don't have a pipeline, you're paying for the wrong half.

## The volume question

Count the grant applications you submitted in the last twelve months.

**Fewer than about eight**, and one person handles all of them: you need a funder database and a calendar. GrantStation at roughly $199 a year through TechSoup does the first job, and the second is a shared calendar you already pay for. That's a $1,900 annual saving on a workflow you were managing by hand anyway.

**More than about fifteen**, or multiple people writing: the deadline tracking is the product. Missed reporting deadlines cost renewals, and renewals are where grant revenue actually compounds. Stay.

**In between** is the genuinely hard call, and it comes down to whether anyone would notice if the pipeline lived in a spreadsheet. If your current spreadsheet works, keep it and downgrade. If it has already caused one missed deadline, the subscription is cheaper than the grant you lost.

## What no alternative replicates

Be clear about what you'd be giving up. Instrumentl's matching surfaces funders you would not have thought to search for — it works forward from your program profile rather than waiting for the right keyword. A database answers the question you asked. Matching proposes questions.

That difference is worth the most to a shop expanding into new funders and the least to a shop stewarding an established portfolio. If your last five grants came from foundations you already had relationships with, matching isn't earning its keep.

## The cheapest path most shops miss

Candid's Foundation Directory is free, on site, at hundreds of Funding Information Network partner libraries. Not a trial — the full database, at the desk, at no cost. For a small shop that needs to research fifteen foundations properly before a fall application push, a few afternoons at the library replaces a subscription entirely.

It is a genuine inconvenience and it does not scale to ongoing prospecting. But paying several hundred dollars a year for search access when the same database is free twenty minutes away rarely survives contact with a finance committee.

## A note on stacking

These are not mutually exclusive, and the cheapest working setup for many small shops is GrantStation for broad discovery, the library for deep 990 research on the funders that survive the first cut, and a spreadsheet for deadlines. That's about $199 a year. It costs you time instead of money, which is the right trade when you have more of one than the other.
