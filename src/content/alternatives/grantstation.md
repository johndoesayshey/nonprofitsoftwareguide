GrantStation is cheap, broad, and passive. Shops leave it when the volume of work outgrows what a search box can support — not because the data is wrong, but because the spreadsheet around it has started failing.

## The trigger is volume, not dissatisfaction

Count the applications you submitted in the last twelve months, and count the reporting deadlines you're carrying.

**Under about eight applications**, handled by one person: GrantStation plus a calendar is a complete system. There is nothing to fix. At roughly $199 a year you are spending less than the survey mean for shops under $1M raised, and any upgrade is a discretionary purchase of convenience.

**Past about fifteen**, or more than one person writing: the tracking is the problem. Missed reporting deadlines cost renewals, and renewals are where grant revenue actually compounds. That is what [Instrumentl](/go/instrumentl) is priced for — the matching engine gets the attention, but the deadline pipeline is what earns the subscription back.

**In between**: ask whether your spreadsheet has already caused a missed deadline. If it has, the subscription is cheaper than the grant you lost. If it hasn't, wait.

## What an upgrade actually buys

Two distinct things, and it's worth being clear which one you need.

**Matching** works forward from your program profile and proposes funders you wouldn't have searched for. A database waits to be asked. If your last five grants came from foundations you already had relationships with, matching isn't earning its keep and you have a stewardship program, not a prospecting problem.

**Pipeline** is deadlines, statuses, assigned owners and report dates in one shared place. This is the part that fails silently in a spreadsheet, and it fails worst in exactly the situation where grant revenue matters most — several people writing, several funders reporting on different schedules.

## The cheapest fix most shops miss

If your complaint is research depth rather than volume, don't buy a subscription. Candid's Foundation Directory is free, on site, at hundreds of Funding Information Network partner libraries — the full database, at the desk, no cost. Two afternoons before your fall application push will do the diligence on fifteen foundations properly.

Keeping GrantStation for broad discovery, using the library for deep 990 work on funders that survive the first cut, and running deadlines in a shared spreadsheet is a complete grant research setup for about $199 a year. It costs time instead of money, which is the right trade when you have more of one than the other.

## Reading the 990 either way

Whichever tool you use, the discipline matters more than the source. A foundation's stated priorities are marketing; its 990 giving history is behaviour. When the two disagree, the giving history is the truth — and applying against the stated priorities is how a strong proposal gets declined for reasons nobody ever explains to you.
