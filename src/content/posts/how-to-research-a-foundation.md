Vetting a foundation before you apply takes about 20 minutes and saves a week of wasted writing. Check four things in order: do they accept unsolicited proposals, do they fund your issue and geography, what do they actually give (the 990, not the website), and can you find a warm connection. Most funders should fail the check.

## The 20-minute check, in order

Run the checks in sequence and stop the moment a funder fails one. The cheapest disqualifiers come first.

**1. Do they accept unsolicited proposals?** Many foundations fund only invited or existing grantees, and the website or 990 usually says so. No amount of proposal quality overcomes invitation-only. A closed door is not a dead funder; it moves to the relationship track instead of the proposal track.

**2. Do they fund your issue and your geography?** Most foundations restrict giving to specific fields and regions. [Instrumentl](/go/instrumentl) filters both at once, and its 14-day free trial is enough time to vet a season's worth of prospects ([our Instrumentl review](/platforms/instrumentl/) covers the full feature set). Rule out anyone outside your lane before reading a word of program language; geography kills more applications than quality does.

**3. What do they actually fund?** Open the 990: free on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer, or in more depth through [Candid](/go/candid)'s Foundation Directory, free on-site at hundreds of partner libraries. Check three things against your ask.

- **Grant sizes.** If their checks cluster at $10-25k and you need $100k, that is a cultivation-stage funder, not a lead-gift prospect.
- **Grantee types.** Check whether the grantees look like your organization or like hospitals and universities with familiar names. Some funders' education giving goes entirely to institutions with endowments.
- **Recency and pattern.** Three years of filings, minimum. A three-year pattern is a real priority; one year proves nothing. When the giving contradicts the website's stated priorities, [believe the 990](/blog/funder-priorities-vs-990/).

**4. Can you find a connection?** A board member, a shared funder, a peer organization they already support. Warm beats cold by a wide margin in foundation giving. Check your board list against their trustee list before every approach; it takes two minutes and occasionally it is gold.

## Turn the check into a scorecard

The value is the discipline: 20 minutes to decide whether to spend a week. Keep four columns: accepts proposals, fits issue and geography, giving range matches, connection. Only funders passing all four earn a proposal. Funders passing three get logged with a note on what is missing. Everyone else gets dropped without guilt.

Revisit the three-of-four funders quarterly. Boards turn over, programs launch, and a connection you lacked in March can surface by September. The logged notes make the second look a five-minute job instead of a fresh 20 minutes.

Done consistently, this is the difference between a grant program that chases and one that wins: not better writing, just declining to write for funders whose own filings said no.
