A grant calendar a two-person team can keep needs six columns: funder, ask amount, deadline, status, owner, and next action. Review it once a week at a standing time. That is the entire system. Most grant calendars fail from tracking too much, not too little, and die quietly within a quarter.

## The six columns

- **Funder.** One row per funder per opportunity.
- **Ask.** The dollar amount, so the sheet doubles as a pipeline forecast.
- **Deadline.** Application deadlines and report deadlines, on the same sheet.
- **Status.** Keep the list short: researching, drafting, submitted, awarded, declined, reporting. More states means more rows filed wrong.
- **Owner.** A name, never "team." Unowned rows are how deadlines slip.
- **Next action.** The single concrete step and the date it happens. This column is the difference between a calendar and a to-do list nobody reads.

If a column does not change a decision at the weekly review, cut it. Probability percentages, contact logs, and elaborate color codes are where two-person calendars go to die.

## The weekly review

Fifteen minutes, same time every week, both people present. Walk the sheet top to bottom and confirm two things per row: the last next action got done, and a new one is written down. Anything due within 30 days gets said out loud. The review is what keeps the calendar true; a sheet nobody reads aloud drifts from reality in about three weeks.

## Keep report deadlines on the sheet

Won grants generate deadlines too. A missed report damages a funder relationship faster than a declined proposal ever will, so reporting dates live in the same calendar with the same owner column, not in someone's inbox. ## When the spreadsheet stops working

Three signals. You are prospecting for new funders every month and pasting research into the sheet by hand. The same funder sits at multiple stages at once (an open application, an active grant, a report due) and one row cannot hold it. Or a deadline slips not because nobody cared but because the sheet was stale. Any one is survivable; two together mean the tool is now the bottleneck.
