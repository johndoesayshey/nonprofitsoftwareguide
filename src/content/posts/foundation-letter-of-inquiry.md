A letter of inquiry is not a compressed proposal. It is a screening document, and the program officer reading it is trying to answer one question quickly: is this worth a full review?

Writing it as a miniature proposal — problem statement, methodology, evaluation plan, sustainability — is the most common mistake, and it fails because it answers questions nobody has asked yet.

## What is actually being checked

**Are you eligible?** Geography, organization type, program area, sometimes budget size. This is a yes/no, and it is the first thing checked. If you cannot state clearly and early that you are within their guidelines, you get screened out before anything else is read.

**Do you fit what they actually fund?** Not what their website says they fund — what their grant list shows. Program officers know their own portfolio, and an applicant who obviously does not resemble it reads as someone who did no research.

**Is the amount sensible?** A request far above their median grant answers itself.

**Can you write clearly?** Uncomfortable but real. An LOI is a two-page writing sample, and a program officer extrapolating from it to a twenty-page proposal is making a reasonable inference about how much work you will be.

**Did you follow the instructions?** Length, format, deadline, what to include. This is the cheapest screen a funder has, and plenty of applications fail it.

## The structure that works

**Open with the ask, not the mission.** One sentence: who you are, what you do, what you want, how much. A program officer reading fifty of these should know within ten seconds what is being requested. Save the mission statement — they will read the rest if the first sentence earns it.

**One short paragraph on the need**, specific to your community and, where possible, with a number attached. Not the national statistic. The local one.

**One paragraph on what you will do with the money.** Concrete. Who does what, for how many people, over what period. Resist listing every activity — pick the spine of the program.

**One paragraph on why you.** Track record, not aspiration. What you have already done that makes this credible.

**Two sentences on fit.** This is the paragraph most LOIs omit and the one that most often earns an invitation. Name why *this* funder — something specific from their giving history or stated interest that connects to your work. It demonstrates the research the rest of the letter implies.

**Close with the amount, the total program cost, and a clear next step.** "We are requesting $25,000 toward a $92,000 program and would welcome the opportunity to submit a full proposal."

## What to leave out

The founding story. The full board list. Attachments nobody asked for. Adjectives.

Also leave out anything that sounds like it was written for a different funder. Recycling is inevitable and fine, but a letter mentioning priorities this foundation does not have is worse than a generic one — it proves you are sending the same thing everywhere.

## The research that has to happen first

An LOI cannot fix a bad fit, and fit is visible before you write.

Pull the funder's 990 grant list. Check three things: whether organizations like yours appear on it, what the **median** grant is, and whether the list repeats year over year. A grant list that is 90% identical across three years is a closed system funding existing relationships, and a cold LOI there is a low-probability shot no matter how well written. ## After you send it

Log it: funder, date, amount, program, expected response window. Then leave it alone.

If the guidelines state a response time, wait it out before following up once. If they say you will only hear if invited, that is the whole process — do not follow up at all, and do not read silence as anything other than a decline.

An invitation is not an award. Invited proposals still get declined. But the fit question has been settled in your favour, which is the hardest part, and the odds from there are far better than a cold submission ever offers.
