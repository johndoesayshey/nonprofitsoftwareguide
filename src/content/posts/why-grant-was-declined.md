Decline letters are written to close the conversation without giving offence, which is why they all say the same thing: many worthy applications, limited resources, we encourage you to apply again.

That sentence is usually true and almost never the reason. Here are the reasons, roughly in order of how often they apply.

## You were not a fit, and it was visible before you wrote

This is the largest category by a wide margin, and it is entirely preventable.

A foundation's stated priorities are marketing copy. Its 990 giving history is behaviour — who it actually funded, for how much, where, over several years. When the two disagree, the giving history is the truth.

The mismatches that produce declines are usually obvious in hindsight. A funder whose stated interest is "education" but whose entire grant list is scholarships at three universities. A regional funder with a geographic footprint you sit just outside. A funder whose largest grant in four years was $10,000, receiving a $60,000 request.

None of those are writing problems. They are research problems, and the research takes twenty minutes per funder.

## You asked for the wrong amount

Pull the funder's grant list and find the median. Not the largest grant — the median, because the largest is usually a long relationship or a board member's cause.

If the median is $12,000 and you asked for $50,000, you were declined for arithmetic. Some funders will counter-offer at their usual size. Most will not, because reducing an ask by 75% requires a conversation they do not have time for with an unknown applicant.

The first grant from a new funder is almost always smaller than you want. Ask at their size, deliver well, and ask for more next time.

## They fund organizations they know

Uncomfortable and true. A large share of foundation giving goes to organizations with an existing relationship, whether through a board connection, a past grant, or a program officer who has visited.

This does not make cold applications pointless — new organizations enter grant lists every year. It does mean a cold application to a funder with a stable, repeating grant list is a low-probability shot, and the giving history will show you which funders those are. A list that is 90% identical year over year is telling you something.

Where a funder accepts inquiry calls or letters of inquiry, that is the cheap way to convert a cold application into a warm one. Take it.

## The budget did not survive scrutiny

Reviewers read the budget first. Round numbers everywhere, personnel with no FTE basis, a total that does not match the ask, or a request for zero indirect costs all cost credibility before the narrative is read.

The zero-indirect one catches people out because it feels generous. To a program officer it reads as not understanding your own cost structure. More on [what a grant budget actually has to show](/blog/grant-budget-what-to-include/).

## They doubted you could deliver

Capacity, not merit. A $200,000 organization asking for a $150,000 program grant is asking a funder to nearly double it in one year, which is a real risk to them and to you.

Similarly, a proposal describing four new staff, a new site and a new partnership, from an organization with three employees, invites the question of who is going to run all this. Ambition reads as inexperience when it outruns evidence.

## What to actually do next

**Ask, once, and do not argue.** A short note thanking them and asking whether they can share anything that would help a future application sometimes gets a real answer. Program officers remember which applicants asked for information and which contested the decision.

**Log it.** Funder, date, amount, program, outcome, and any reason given. Two years of that data will tell you more about your hit rate than any individual decline. **Check the fit again, honestly.** Go back to the 990. If the decline was a fit problem, reapplying will produce the same result no matter how much you improve the writing.

**Reapply where there is a reason to.** Many funders expect it, and a second application that reflects something learned lands differently from a resubmission. If nothing has changed — not the program, not the fit, not the amount — wait until something has.

## The number worth tracking

Not how many grants you won. What share of applications went to funders whose giving history actually matched your organization.

Most small shops have a poor hit rate because they apply widely rather than because they write badly.
