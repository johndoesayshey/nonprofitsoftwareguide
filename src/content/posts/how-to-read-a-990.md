A foundation's Form 990-PF answers four questions its website may not: who they actually funded, how much they gave, who sits on the board, and whether they accept applications. Read the grants schedule first, then the application information, then the trustee list, then assets. Twenty minutes per funder is enough.

## The grants schedule: read it first

The list of grants paid is the heart of the 990-PF. It shows who received money, how much, and often a one-line purpose. Pull four things from it.

- **Typical grant size.** If checks cluster far below your intended ask, adjust the ask or move on.
- **Grantee types.** Look for organizations shaped like yours, not just organizations in your field. A funder whose education giving all lands at universities is not a K-12 nonprofit's prospect.
- **Repeat grantees.** A schedule dominated by the same names year after year signals a relationship-driven funder. Plan a warm introduction, not a cold proposal.
- **Geography.** Where the money lands matters more than where the foundation is headquartered.

Read three years of schedules. One year can be an anomaly; three years is a pattern.

## The application information

The filing states whether the foundation accepts unsolicited applications, and if so, the format, deadlines, and contact. This is the fastest disqualifier in prospect research. A foundation that funds only pre-selected organizations is a relationship prospect, not a proposal prospect, and knowing that before you write saves the whole week.

## The trustee list

The officers and trustees section is your connection map. Run the names against your board, your donors, and your professional network before every approach. It also signals direction: new trustees often precede new funding priorities, so a changed board with an unchanged website is a reason to weight recent giving over stated interests.

## The assets

Asset size is a rough predictor of future grant budgets. Private foundations are required to distribute a minimum share of assets each year, so a growing corpus generally means a growing grants budget, and a shrinking one means a smaller pool even if last year's giving looked strong. You are not doing accounting here; you are checking trajectory.

## A reading order that holds

Grants schedule, application information, trustees, assets. Stop at the first disqualifier and move to the next funder.
