This is the category where nonprofits overspend by buying one impressive tool when two cheap ones would each do their job.

## The two jobs

**Intake**, applications, volunteer sign-ups, event registrations, grant submissions. Structured data arriving from people outside your organization. This is a forms problem, and [Jotform](/go/jotform) solves it for about $17 a month for the whole account with the nonprofit discount.

**Workflow**, who is doing what, by when, and what is blocked. Program delivery, campaign calendars, grant pipelines, board tasks. This is a boards-and-owners problem, and [monday.com](/go/monday) solves it from about $9 a seat.

Together that is roughly $750–$950 a year for a small team. Our 2026 survey puts mean spend in this category at $175 under $250K raised and $572 at $250K–$1M, so a full setup sits slightly above the median band, which is defensible, because this is the category where the tools actually save staff time, not just produce reports.

## The per-seat trap

Headline prices mislead here more than anywhere else on this site, because the pricing units differ.

| | Unit | Five-person team |
|---|---|---|
| Jotform Bronze | per **account** | ~$204/yr |
| monday.com Standard | per **seat** | ~$720/yr |
| Airtable Team | per **seat** | $1,200/yr |

Jotform pricing the account, not the seat, is the single most underrated fact in this category. Ten people touching forms costs exactly what one person costs. Count seats before you compare anything.

## Workflow tool or database

The other decision, and the one people get wrong.

[Airtable](/platforms/airtable/) is a genuine relational database and it is excellent at being one. It also expects you to design the system, which means what gets built reflects one person's mental model, and when that person leaves, their successor inherits a structure nobody can explain. It is the same failure mode as an over-configured CRM, and nonprofit turnover makes it more likely here, not less.

monday.com ships with the structure already decided. Less capable, and considerably more likely to still be in use in two years.

Buy Airtable when you have genuinely relational data *and* a named person who will own it. If the person is aspirational, not actual, buy the simpler thing.

## The failure mode to plan for

Operations tools die quietly. Nothing breaks when people stop using them, the work just drifts back to email and spreadsheets, and six months later you are paying for a system nobody opens.

Name an owner before you buy, and put a recurring monthly review in that person's calendar. That single administrative step does more for the return on this category than any feature comparison on this page.
