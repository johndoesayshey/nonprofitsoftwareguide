Food banks have a revenue mix that almost no donor CRM is designed around: a large base of small individual gifts, a corporate and foundation grant program that is usually the bigger number, and a stream of in-kind food donations that never appears in any dollar total but is most of what goes out the door.

## Start by naming your biggest revenue line

This is the question that decides which software to buy first, and it's routinely answered wrong.

If grants and corporate support are the larger share — and for most food banks they are — then a grant research and tracking tool is a better first purchase than a donor CRM. A spreadsheet holds a donor list for a long time. It fails much faster at a grant pipeline with reporting deadlines across a dozen funders, and the cost of that failure is a renewal you don't get.

[GrantStation](/go/grantstation) is about $199 a year through TechSoup and is the cheapest credible entry. Our 2026 survey put mean grant research spend at $250 a year for shops under $250K raised and $746 at $250K–$1M, which tells you most of the sector is spending at roughly that level.

Buy the donor CRM when individual giving is big enough that losing donors costs more than the subscription.

## In-kind gifts belong in the donor record

A local grocery chain that donates forty thousand pounds of product a year is a major donor. If that shows up only in warehouse inventory and never in the development database, then your top-donor list is wrong, your stewardship plan is wrong, and the person who could ask them for a cash gift doesn't know to.

Every system on this page can record a non-cash gift. What varies is whether it lands in giving history in a way you can report on, and what the acknowledgement letter says — the substantiation rules for non-cash gifts are different from cash, and a system that just writes a dollar figure into the cash field will generate letters that are technically wrong.

Test it during the trial with a real example. Record a donated pallet of food with a fair market value, then look at the donor's record and the generated letter.

## The seasonality problem

Food banks routinely take a third or more of annual individual revenue in the last six weeks of the year. Two consequences follow, and both are operational rather than technical.

The acknowledgement process has to survive a surge. Whatever takes two days in April takes three weeks in December, and a thank-you that arrives in February doesn't convert anyone.

And your retention work happens in February. The December donor who gave because of a news story about need is exactly the person who lapses, and the only thing that prevents it is a specific second ask at a moment when nobody in the building is thinking about fundraising. That is what a task-driven system is for, and it's why [DonorDock](/go/donordock)'s ActionBoard leads this page over systems with longer feature lists.

## Corporate relationships are not individuals

A regional grocery chain might donate product weekly, sponsor your spring drive, run an employee giving campaign, and give through its corporate foundation. That is one relationship with four gift types and several human contacts.

If your system can only hold individuals, that fragments into four records and nobody ever sees the total. Ask specifically about organizational records with related individual contacts, and about whether you can report on the organization's total support across gift types. It's a boring question that decides whether you can walk into a renewal meeting with an accurate number.
