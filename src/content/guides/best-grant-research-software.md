Grant research is the best return on a fundraising software dollar for most shops, and also the category where it is easiest to overspend by a factor of ten. The products are separated by whether they manage a pipeline, not by how good their data is.

## The question that decides it

How many grant applications did you submit in the last twelve months?

**Fewer than about eight**, one person handling all of them: you need a funder database and a calendar. [GrantStation](/go/grantstation) at roughly $199 through TechSoup does the first; a shared calendar you already pay for does the second. That is a $1,900 annual saving on a workflow you were managing by hand anyway.

**More than about fifteen**, or several people writing: the pipeline is the product. Missed reporting deadlines cost renewals, and renewals are where grant revenue actually compounds. [Instrumentl](/go/instrumentl) is priced for exactly this and earns it back on one saved relationship.

**In between** is the genuinely hard call. Ask whether anyone would notice if the pipeline lived in a spreadsheet. If your current one works, keep it. If it has already caused a missed deadline, the subscription is cheaper than the grant you lost.

## What the sector actually spends

From our 2026 survey of 100 U.S. fundraising professionals, mean annual grant research spend:

- **Under $250K raised**, $250
- **$250K–$1M**, $746
- **$1M–$5M**, $2,278
- **$5M+**, $5,713

Instrumentl's entry tier is $2,148 a year. That sits above the band for everyone under $1M, which does not make it wrong; it makes it a purchase that has to be justified by grants won rather than by convenience.

## The free path most shops miss

Candid's Foundation Directory is available in full, on site, at no cost, at hundreds of Funding Information Network partner libraries. The complete database, at the desk.

For a small shop that needs to research fifteen foundations properly before a fall push, a couple of scheduled afternoons replaces a subscription entirely. The catch is real, research that has to be scheduled tends not to happen, and that inconvenience is the honest argument for paying. But paying several hundred dollars a year for search access when the same database is free twenty minutes away rarely survives a finance committee.

The cheapest complete setup for a small shop: GrantStation for discovery, the library for diligence on funders that survive the first cut, and a spreadsheet for deadlines. About $199 a year. It costs time instead of money, which is the right trade when you have more of one than the other.

## Reading the 990, whichever tool you use

The discipline matters more than the source. A foundation's stated priorities are marketing. Its 990 giving history is behaviour.

When those disagree, the giving history is the truth, and applying against the stated priorities is how a strong proposal gets declined for reasons nobody ever explains to you. Every tool here puts grant history in front of you. Whether you read it before writing is the part that decides your hit rate.
